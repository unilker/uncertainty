## Abstract

Accredited laboratories evaluate measurement uncertainty with spreadsheets or with programs that cover one part of
the Guide to the Expression of Uncertainty in Measurement (GUM) and its supplements, and they learn and check their
methods on the worked examples of guidance documents. KAPSAM is an open-source program with a Rust engine, a
command-line tool and a desktop and mobile application. It implements the GUM with its 2026 amendment, the Monte Carlo
methods of its supplements, conformity assessment, top-down evaluation, proficiency testing, calibration with
uncertainty in both variables, precision experiments and further methods of the sector guides. The program is verified
on 286 projects from the worked examples of 37 sources, 25 of them guidance documents and standards: its test suite
recomputes all 1865 reference values at every change of the code, Suncal reproduces the results of 166 projects and
spreadsheet formulas recalculated in LibreOffice those of 189. Recomputing the examples produced 100 findings. Eleven
are confirmed inconsistencies that change a final printed result, five of them by more than a tenth of its expanded
uncertainty; a metrologist who had not seen the code reproduced them from the documents. We argue that worked examples
should be published with machine-readable inputs and checked by independent recomputation.

**Keywords:** measurement uncertainty; GUM; Monte Carlo method; software verification; worked examples; reference
data; ISO/IEC 17025

## 1. Introduction

ISO/IEC 17025 requires testing and calibration laboratories to evaluate the uncertainty of their results (7.6), to
validate their software before use (7.11.2) and to check calculations and data transfers in a systematic way (7.11.6)
[1]. The evaluation itself rests on the *Guide to the expression of uncertainty in measurement* (GUM) and its
supplements: the law of propagation of uncertainty (LPU) [2], the Monte Carlo method for one output (JCGM 101) [3] and
for several outputs (JCGM 102) [4], the use of uncertainty in conformity assessment (JCGM 106) [5], and, since 2026, an
amendment of the GUM on nonlinear models [6] and a new part with worked examples (GUM-5) [7]. Sector guides translate
this framework: the Eurachem/CITAC guides for analytical chemistry [8, 9], the Nordtest handbooks for environmental
laboratories [10, 11], EA-4/02 [12], UKAS M3003 [13] and the EURAMET calibration guides [14, 15] for calibration,
ILAC G17 for testing [16], and ISO standards for proficiency testing [17], precision experiments [18, 19] and
gas analysis [20].

Each of these documents teaches by example. A laboratory that sets up a budget for a new method compares its numbers
with a worked example, and a laboratory that verifies its software reruns worked examples whose results are printed.
Both uses assume that the printed results follow from the printed inputs. In our experience, and in that of the
developers of other programs (Section 6.4), they often do not exactly, and occasionally they do not at all.

**Software and its verification.** The NIST Uncertainty Machine [21, 22] evaluates explicit models by the GUM and
by Monte Carlo; Sandia's Suncal, a Python package [23, 24], adds symbolic LPU, curve fitting and decision risk for
calibration; the GUM Tree Calculator [25] and METAS UncLib [26] propagate "uncertain numbers" through programs; GUM
Workbench [27] is a widely used commercial program for model-based budgets; the NIST Consensus Builder (NICOB)
[28] combines interlaboratory results; MUkit [29] implements the Nordtest approach to top-down evaluation, whose ISO
counterpart is ISO 11352 [30]; the MUPR spreadsheet [31] implements a top-down evaluation for chemistry; and
UncertCalc [32] combines a top-down evaluation of quality-control data with a bottom-up budget for clinical
laboratories. A survey of 35 programs by the European Metrology Network Mathmet found that only 54 % give some evidence
of validation and that support for JCGM 102 is the largest gap [33]. The verification of such programs has been
studied before. At PTB, the validation of a GUM-supporting program included the reproduction of the examples in the
GUM appendices, with several hundred black-box test cases that checked each output for its value, its number of
significant digits and its rounding [34]; Greif and Schrepf derived reusable test cases for the GUM conformity of
software and listed the statements of the GUM that cannot be tested or are ambiguous [35]; and the TraCIM
service validates metrology software against reference data sets with known results, for example the evaluation
software of coordinate measuring machines [36]. Errors in the worked examples themselves have been reported
case by case, in published errata [37] and by the authors of other programs [23].

This work extends that approach in scale and in kind: the worked examples of 25 guidance documents and standards, and
of 12 further sources, are converted into machine-readable projects with a documented tolerance for every reference
value, recomputed at every change of the code, compared with independent implementations, and every discrepancy is
classified. Its contributions, which also give the structure of the paper, are:

1. **The program, KAPSAM** (Sections 2 and 3), which covers the GUM suite and the main sector guides, cites the clause
   behind every algorithm, and traces every result to its inputs, its method and the exact build of the software.
2. **Its verification** (Sections 4 and 5): a library of 286 self-checking projects with 1865 reference values,
   checks of the reference values and tolerances themselves, and comparisons with an independent program, with
   spreadsheet formulas and with Monte Carlo propagation.
3. **What the recomputation revealed** about the worked examples (Section 6): 100 findings, classified by kind and by
   their effect on the printed result, the confirmed inconsistencies recomputed by a person who had not seen the code.

Section 7 summarises how the program serves the requirements of ISO/IEC 17025, Section 8 discusses what the
verification does and does not show, and Section 9 concludes.

## 2. Evaluation methods

### 2.1 Overview

Terms follow the International Vocabulary of Metrology (VIM) [38] and the GUM [2]. A project contains the input data
of one of eleven evaluation types (Table 1). Each type implements the cited clauses of its guidance document; where a
document leaves a choice open, the choice is an option of the project and is named in the report. The types cover the
routes to an uncertainty that Désenfant and Priel distinguish: the modelling approach of the GUM, which they regard as
the reference method, and the approaches from single-laboratory validation data, from interlaboratory precision
studies and from proficiency testing [39]; these rest on a statistical model, a simplification of the measurement
model in which some input quantities are merged [40]. A laboratory can therefore evaluate a method by more than one
route with the same program; an uncertainty from a model that is much smaller than the spread seen in
interlaboratory comparisons points to a missing component [39]. The equations that the guidance does not state, or
states differently, are given in Appendix A.

| several outputs | LPU and Monte Carlo for several outputs, explicit and implicit models, coverage regions | [4] |

### 2.2 Measurement models and the GUM

Models are written as expressions; the parser differentiates them symbolically, so that sensitivity coefficients are
exact and can be printed. The combined standard uncertainty includes correlations. The second-order term of the
estimate of the 2026 amendment of the GUM [6] is always computed and added on request; with it the variance includes
the second- and third-order terms for correlated normal inputs of Mana and Pennecchi [41] (Appendix A.1). Inputs
evaluated from the same data can be declared as an *ensemble*, which enters the Welch–Satterthwaite formula as one
component with its common degrees of freedom (Willink [50]; the GUM Tree Calculator implements the same rule [25];
Appendix A.2). Expanded uncertainties are rounded by the rule of EA-4/02 (ordinary rounding unless it lowers U by more
than 5 %, then upwards) [12], by the rule of the Eurachem/CITAC MUPR guide [9] or always upwards; the statement names
the rule. Calibration reports can apply the calibration and measurement capability (CMC) as a lower limit, since the
reported uncertainty may not be smaller than the CMC (ILAC P14 [51]).

### 2.3 Monte Carlo method

The adaptive procedure of JCGM 101 7.9 stops when the estimate, the standard uncertainty and both end points of the
coverage interval are stable to the stated numerical tolerance, which the report gives; with a fixed number of trials
the report gives the number and the seed. Besides the probabilistically symmetric and the shortest coverage interval,
the program reports the half-width U of the interval symmetric about the estimate, Pr(|Y − y| ≤ U) = p. The GUM result
is validated against the Monte Carlo result (JCGM 101 clause 8); when inputs from repeated observations are present,
the report states that the two evaluations use different input variances for few degrees of freedom (s/√n against
√(ν/(ν − 2))·s/√n), so that a failed validation need not indicate an error.

### 2.4 Conformity assessment and proficiency testing

Conformity assessment gives the conformance probability of each result and, for a process distribution, the global
consumer's and producer's risks of JCGM 106 [5]; the decision rule and any guard band are stated in the report, as
ILAC G8 recommends [43]. Guard bands can be derived from the test uncertainty ratio TUR = (T_U − T_L)/(2U₉₅),
including the managed guard band of Dobbert [52], whose acceptance limits lie outside the tolerance above
TUR = 4.59 unless the factor is limited; the program uses U₉₅ = k₉₅u by default (Suncal uses 2u), keeps 2u as an
option and limits the factor to 1 unless an agreed rule allows more. Such rules are conventions: Pendrill [53]
examines them in economic terms and Forbes [54] derives acceptance limits from the costs of wrong decisions; the
program does not optimise costs, but it reports the risks that such an optimisation needs. For proficiency testing the
program computes the robust statistics, scores and homogeneity checks of ISO 13528 [17]; the modified Algorithm A of
Carobbi, which also uses the stated uncertainties [55], and the criteria of Frahm and Wright, which classify results
with a large comparison uncertainty as inconclusive [56], are not implemented.

### 2.5 Consensus values and results near a physical bound

The consensus module follows the NIST Consensus Builder [28, 49] and was checked against its R source code, which is
public. It covers the two causes of mutually inconsistent results that Shirono et al. distinguish — an effect common
to most laboratories, which calls for a random-effects model with a dark uncertainty [48], and outlying laboratories
[57] — and gives the DerSimonian–Laird estimate [58] with the Knapp–Hartung adjustment [59] and with the parametric
bootstrap of NICOB, which draws the between-laboratory variance from the gamma approximation of Cochran's Q of
Biggerstaff and Tweedie [60]. Reading the code showed two details that its manual does not state (Appendix A.3); the
program follows the code. The hierarchical Bayesian model with a half-Cauchy prior on the between-laboratory standard
deviation is evaluated on deterministic grids instead of by MCMC (Appendix A.4). Degrees of equivalence are available
in the version of the CIPM MRA and in the leave-one-out version, whose uncertainty uses the modified Knapp–Hartung
adjustment of Röver et al. [61] as NICOB does, unilateral and bilateral.

A mass fraction cannot exceed 1 g/g. When y ± U crosses such a bound, the program truncates the interval at the bound
and states the result as y +U_U/−U_L, as the IUPAC technical report on purity assignment allows [62]; with the Monte
Carlo method it also reports the distribution restricted to the bound, which corresponds to a rectangular prior on the
measurand [63], with its mean, standard deviation and coverage interval. Cowen and Ellison compare these options for
results near natural limits [64]; the report states which was used.

## 3. Software design and implementation

KAPSAM (Turkish for *coverage*, as in coverage factor and coverage interval) is organised around one engine, so that
every interface gives the same numbers (Figure 1). The engine is a Rust library (`core`, about 13 000 lines) with a
project layer (`project`, about 19 000 lines) that reads project files, runs the evaluations, checks results against
reference values and writes reports; a command-line tool (`cli`) serves batch work, and a C interface (`ffi`)
connects the engine to an application written in Flutter (about 9 000 lines of Dart) for Windows, Linux, macOS,
Android and iOS. The program has no network code: all calculations run on the user's computer.

<!-- fig1 -->

A project is a JSON file that holds everything needed to repeat an evaluation: bilingual metadata (title, explanation,
notes and sources with section and link), the input data of one evaluation type and, optionally, reference values
with tolerances and a statement of where they come from. The file of the first Eurachem example, shortened:

```
{ "format": "uncertainty-calculations/project", "version": 1,
  "metadata": { "title": { "en": "Preparation of a cadmium calibration standard", "tr": "…" },
                "sources": [ { "document": "EURACHEM/CITAC Guide CG 4 (QUAM:2012.P1)",
                               "section": "Example A1, Tables A1.2 and A1.3" } ] },
  "model": { "output": { "name": "c_Cd", "unit": "mg/L", "expression": "1000 * m * P / V" },
             "inputs": [ { "name": "P", "distribution":
                             { "type": "rectangular", "value": 0.9999, "half_width": 0.0001 } },
                         { "name": "m", "unit": "mg", "distribution":
                             { "type": "normal", "mean": 100.28, "sd": 0.05 } }, … ] },
  "analysis": { "gum": { "coverage_factor": 2 }, "mcm": { "trials": 1000000, "seed": 1 } },
  "expected": { "gum": { "standard_uncertainty": { "value": 0.8633, "tolerance": 0.001 } },
                "reference": { "en": "QUAM:2012 Table A1.3 (spreadsheet value u = 0.8633 mg/L).", … } } }
```

The guide prints the spreadsheet (Kragten) value 0.8633 mg/L; the analytical value is 0.8637 mg/L, so the tolerance is
wider than half a unit in the last digit, and the reason is stated in the file (Section 4.2). The schema rejects
unknown fields, so that a misspelt option is an error rather than silently ignored. Results are never stored in the
file; they are recomputed from the inputs. With its reference values, a project is its own test case: `kapsam check`
evaluates it and compares every result with its reference.

Derivatives of first and second order are formed symbolically and simplified before evaluation. The random-number
generator is the enhanced Wichmann–Hill generator recommended by JCGM 101 [3, 65], checked against its primary source;
normal deviates come from the Box–Muller transform, which is exact, and gamma deviates from the method of Marsaglia and
Tsang [66]. In a model with several outputs every trial evaluates all outputs from one draw of the joint input
distribution. All calculations are done in double precision; values are rounded only in the reported statement.

Quality assurance of the software: 222 automated tests of the engine, including one that evaluates all 286 library
projects, and 28 tests of the application run at every change of the source code (continuous integration), together
with a formatting check, a static analysis that treats every warning as an error and the recalculation of the
spreadsheet verification workbooks in LibreOffice Calc [67] (the version of the integration server's operating
system, 26.2 at the time of writing; 24.2 for the supplementary material). The packages for the five platforms are built from
the same commit. Every report states the program version, the git commit and date it was built from, the compiler and
the SHA-256 digest of the project as evaluated (ISO/IEC 17025 7.5, 7.11). The source code, the generators of the
library projects, the scripts of the recomputations and the formulas of the spreadsheet workbooks were written with an
AI coding assistant (Claude, Anthropic) under the author's direction, who chose the methods and the documents, defined
the reference values and tolerances, and reviewed the results; correctness therefore rests on comparisons with values
the assistant did not produce, and Table 8 states what each check shares with the program. The repository contains a
research log of every design decision and finding, the generators that write each library project from the numerical
data of its source, and the scripts of every recomputation.

The application opens on cards for the evaluation types and on the library, which can be searched and filtered by
level. A project has five tabs: an overview, an editor for the model or the data, the settings, the project file
itself, and the results (Figure 2). The interface and the reports exist in English and Turkish; reports are written as
Markdown, self-contained HTML, PDF and JSON with all values in full precision.

## 4. Verification design

### 4.1 The library

The library holds 286 projects (Table 3). Their primary sources are 25 guidance documents and standards with 256
projects — the GUM, its supplements and GUM-5, the Eurachem/CITAC, Nordtest, EA, UKAS and EURAMET guides, five ISO
standards, NIST TN 1900 [68] and the manuals of the NIST Uncertainty Machine and Consensus Builder, the EMPIR EMUE
compendium [69] and the IUPAC brief guide [70] — five BIPM reference data reports on qNMR [71] with 9 projects, five
papers and a book with 19 (Palma et al. [72], Bettencourt da Silva [31], Koepke et al. [49], Merkatas et al. [73],
Possolo and Bodnar [45], Possolo and Meija [74]), and an unpublished procedure of a metrology institute with 2. The
verification claims of this paper rest on the 25 guidance documents and standards; the other sources extend the test
cases. By field, 144 projects are in chemistry, 36 in electrical, 31 in mass, 24 general, 16 in physical quantities,
12 in length, 10 in temperature, 7 in flow and 6 in pressure; by evaluation type, 139 contain a measurement model and
the others cover the remaining ten types (Table 2; the full list with the source of every project is supplementary
material S1). One worked example may give several projects, as variants; the 286 projects reproduce 228 worked
examples or variants.

### 4.2 Reference values and tolerances

A reference value is the value printed in the source, with a tolerance of half a unit in its last digit, wherever the
source reproduces it from its own inputs within its rounding. Where the printed value is itself a Monte Carlo or MCMC
estimate, its sampling error is added to the tolerance. Where a source prints nothing, or prints a value that does not
follow from its inputs, the reference value is an independent recomputation by a script of the repository using NumPy
[75] and SciPy [76], and the discrepancy is recorded as a finding; an engine's own output is never used as its own
reference. Every project states where its reference values come from. Classified from these statements (S1), the
reference values of 207 projects (1239 values) are printed values only, those of 21 projects (149 values) include
printed Monte Carlo or MCMC estimates, and those of 58 projects (477 values) come partly or wholly from an independent
recomputation.

Of the 1865 reference values, 938 have a tolerance of half a unit in the last digit, 244 of up to one unit, 281 are
Monte Carlo values and 402 have a wider tolerance whose reason is stated in the project (for example a value derived
from an input printed with two significant digits). Across all values, |Δ|/tolerance has a median of 0.23 and a 99th
percentile of 0.98. Of the 74 values with |Δ| above 0.9 of their tolerance, 51 have a tolerance of half a unit: their
exact value lies near the rounding limit of the printed one (for example 93.155 printed as 93.16). All 74 are listed
with the basis of their tolerance in supplementary material S3.

A Monte Carlo result is compared with a tolerance of half a unit in the last printed digit plus about five times its
dispersion over seeds. To test that rule, every one of the 61 projects with a Monte Carlo check was run with ten seeds
other than its own (2810 comparisons of 281 values). In a first run, 20 comparisons from 7 values fell outside their
tolerance (at most 1.26 times it): their tolerances had been set from the program's own seed, and for one of them
(GUM-5 Example 14) the Monte Carlo mean differs from the printed GUM estimate by the nonlinearity of the model. Their
tolerances were reset by the rule; all 2810 comparisons then lie within tolerance, with a largest |Δ|/tolerance of
0.98 (S3).

### 4.3 Classification of discrepancies

Before the audit of the findings, four classes were defined: (a) intermediate rounding that changes a printed digit;
(b) a misprint or transcription error without effect on the result; (c) an inconsistency; (n) no discrepancy (a
methodological note or a full reproduction). After a first internal review of the findings, the class-c findings were
further divided by their effect: c1, a final printed result — the estimate, its uncertainty or the stated outcome — is
not reproduced at its printed digits; c2, only an intermediate value is not; m, a difference of method or
interpretation rather than an inconsistency; e, an error already corrected by a published erratum. These subclasses
were introduced after the findings had been collected, and three findings were reclassified later in the light of
independent recomputation and review (Section 6.1). A finding counts as confirmed when the recomputation from the
document's own data leaves no open question; for a c1 finding, in addition, no reasonable rounding of the document's
own intermediate values may give the printed result. Findings that await a check against another source are open and
are not counted.

### 4.4 Independent checks

Every project that Suncal 1.7.1 [24] can express was evaluated by both programs (supplementary material S4): 135
measurement models, 12 models with several outputs, 6 calibrations with uncertainties in both variables, compared
with Suncal's York fit for straight lines and its orthogonal distance regression for the other functions, and 18
conformity assessments, compared by the conformance probability of each result and the global risks. These are 171
comparisons of 166 projects, since five projects are compared both as models and as conformity assessments. The other
project types have no counterpart in Suncal. The reference values of the three projects on guard bands from the test
uncertainty ratio are computed from the published guard-band formulas and the risk integrals of JCGM 106 with SciPy,
not taken from Suncal, so that their comparison with Suncal is not circular.

For measurement models, proficiency tests, precision experiments and top-down evaluations, KAPSAM also writes a
spreadsheet verification workbook (.xlsx). It holds the data of the project and ordinary spreadsheet formulas: the
measurement model as a formula, evaluated at the estimates and at xᵢ ± 0.001 u(xᵢ), so that the sensitivity
coefficients are central differences in the spirit of Kragten's spreadsheet [8, Appendix E]; Algorithm A of ISO 13528
with one column per iteration; the analysis of variance of ISO 5725-2; the components of Nordtest TR 537. KAPSAM's own
results appear only as fixed numbers beside the spreadsheet's, with the difference and a tolerance. The arithmetic is
thus done by the spreadsheet program and every formula can be inspected; the formulas themselves follow the same
reading of the standards as the program (Section 8). Spreadsheets and an R script have been used in the same way to
verify UncertCalc on randomly generated data [32]; built from the library, the workbooks make this check on data whose
results are printed in the guidance.

For the calibrations, the parameter standard uncertainties differ by up to 0.75 % in two projects (EMUE E3.3 and the

function of ISO 6143 B.3, whose parameters b₀ and b₁ have a correlation of −0.999 997, the Monte Carlo standard
deviations of b₀ and b₁ are 1.4 % larger than both linear values, whereas the content of the sample, which depends on
their combination, agrees to 0.04 %.

Of the 4678 values compared in the spreadsheet workbooks, 3684 are computed by spreadsheet formulas; the other 994
repeat an input or a fixed coverage factor and check the transcription of the project into the workbook, not the
arithmetic. The workbooks are recalculated in LibreOffice Calc; they are ordinary .xlsx files, but their recalculation
in Excel has not been checked systematically.

## 6. What the recomputation revealed

### 6.1 Overview

Recomputing the examples produced 100 findings (F1–F100; all listed with their derivation in supplementary material
S2). By class, 27 are intermediate rounding only, 24 misprints without effect, 23 inconsistencies and 13 no
discrepancy; 13 combine two classes (6 a and b, 1 a and c, 6 b and c). Thirty findings have a class-c component; Table 5
divides them by effect and status. Of the 24 that are settled, 11 change a final printed result (c1), 8 only an
intermediate value (c2, two of them partly corrected by errata), 4 are differences of method (m) and 1 is a known
erratum (e); 6 are open and not counted. Counted once each, by their most severe class, the findings concern most of
the documents (Figure 3a): GUM-5 and the Eurachem QUAM carry nine each, UKAS M3003 seven and the EMUE compendium five.
Three findings concern documents outside the 37 sources of the library, which were recomputed while preparing projects
(Magnusson and Ellison [78], Mateos et al. [79], Delker [23]).

### 6.2 Intermediate rounding is common

Thirty-four findings (27 in class a only, 7 together with another class) concern intermediate rounding: a document
rounds a sensitivity coefficient to three digits or a component to two and then combines the rounded numbers, so
that the printed result differs from an exact recomputation in its last digit. This is harmless for the result but
makes the example unusable as a reference to the printed digits, and it hides genuine slips among the rounding
differences. Worked examples should state their inputs unrounded and round only the reported result.

### 6.3 Budget tables that do not reproduce their own components

The BIPM qNMR reference data reports print complete purity budgets [71] (F97). In 7 of the 11 ¹H budget tables of six
reports (nine of the tables are in the library), the printed component of the integral ratio exceeds the product of
the printed sensitivity coefficient and standard uncertainty by 3–17 %; two factors printed with two significant
digits explain up to about 10 %, not all of it. In one table (ISRD-01 Table 6) the printed combined uncertainty,
3.96 × 10⁻⁴, is 3.5 % below the 4.10 × 10⁻⁴ that follows from the printed inputs; whether unrounded spreadsheet values
explain this is open, and the finding is counted as open until it is known. The printed effective degrees of freedom
are reproduced with ν = 4 for the integral ratio in all five tables that give them, whereas the reports describe the
ratio as the mean of four to six replicates or of sixteen values. The certified values are not in question; a budget
whose components cannot be recomputed from its own columns cannot, however, serve as a reference, and recomputation
from the inputs shows this at once.

### 6.4 Monte Carlo and MCMC results are estimates

Printed Monte Carlo and MCMC values carry sampling error, and several discrepancies are of that size: a Bayesian
standard deviation of 0.19 a (years) against the exact 0.185 a (F95), correlations from 5 × 10⁴ draws (F96), a
bootstrap standard uncertainty of 0.77 against 0.752 ng/g from 10⁶ replicates (F99). A deterministic evaluation, where
one exists, removes the ambiguity; where none exists, the number of draws should be printed with the result.

### 6.5 Differences of method

Six class-c findings are differences of method or interpretation. In GUM-5 Example 2 the areas of analyte and internal
standard come from the same three injections, so their joint component of variance has two degrees of freedom by
Willink's ensemble rule [50], which gives ν_eff = 2.11 and k = 4.10; the example applies its eq. (2.3) and obtains
ν_eff = 4.8 and k = 2.6 (F79; both formulas in Appendix A.2). In a simulation of 4 × 10⁵ data sets of three paired
injections with the printed means, standard deviations and correlation, the GUM interval with ν fixed at 4.8 and at
2.11 covers 89 % and 96 %; with ν recomputed from each data set, as a laboratory would, both readings cover 93–94 %
(S2). The choice matters for a single result, but neither reading is an inconsistency; whether eq. (2.3) is the form
that Willink gives for this case remains to be checked, and F79 is open.

Two conventions for t-distributed inputs — the scaled and shifted t of JCGM 101 and the rescaled t of TN 1900 and the
NIST Uncertainty Machine — likewise give different Monte Carlo results for the same data (F31, F35). Huang [80] argues
that the scaled and shifted t should not be used, because its standard deviation, √((n − 1)/(n − 3))·s/√n, exceeds
s/√n and does not exist for fewer than four observations, and proposes a normal distribution with standard deviation
s/(c₄√n); in his example with n = 4 the t convention gives an expanded uncertainty 50.9 % larger. The program follows
JCGM 101 and states the difference in the report (Section 2.3); a comparison of programs for Type A inputs must state
the convention of each.

own, because reproducing published examples verifies the implementation but not the laboratory's procedure.

## 8. Discussion

**Worked examples as reference data.** The examples of guidance documents are de facto the reference data of
uncertainty software, but they are published as typeset text. Converting them to machine-readable form and
recomputing them exposed the problems of Section 6, which careful reading alone had not caught. We propose that issuing
bodies publish the inputs of their examples in a machine-readable form — the project format of Section 3 is one
candidate — and recompute them before publication, as TraCIM does for reference data sets [36]. The library of
this work is offered for that purpose.

**What this verification does and does not show.** Reproducing published values verifies the implementation of
documented methods; it does not validate a laboratory's own measurement procedure. Agreement with the published values
can also hide a common-mode error when the program and a recomputation read a standard the same way. Table 8 therefore
separates two meanings of *independent*: a different implementation, and a different person. The values printed in
the documents, Suncal and the examples of the NIST manuals were produced by others. The NumPy port of the NICOB code,
the scripts of the recomputations and the spreadsheet formulas are different implementations, written in the same
project and with the same AI coding assistant as the program (Section 3), so they guard against arithmetic and coding
errors but not against a misreading of a standard. The c1 findings were recomputed by a metrologist of the author's
institute who is not an author and had not seen the code, from the documents alone and with the intermediate steps
written down. The recomputer used hand calculation, Python and Monte Carlo simulation with the help of an AI assistant
of the same family as the one used to write the program (Claude, Anthropic). The recomputation is therefore independent
of the program's code and of its author, but not of the assistant: a misreading of a document that the assistant tends
to make could be common to both. Against this, most c1 findings rest on a few lines of arithmetic with numbers printed
in the document itself, which a reader can repeat by hand (Table 6); for F78 the document prints the correct
intermediate value itself, and F28 was reported independently by Delker [23]. A review of the program by
metrologists outside the institute has not been made.

**Reporting the findings.** The findings were not communicated to the issuing bodies before submission. The c1
findings will be sent to them as technical notes after acceptance, so that the notes can refer to a peer-reviewed
record, and their replies will be added to the public research log of the program. For ISO 6143, an amendment on the
performance of its program for nonlinear functions is in preparation; whether it addresses F42 cannot be checked
before it is published.

**Limitations.** Not implemented are the NICOB priors for results with finite degrees of freedom, time series and
counting statistics, model averaging, the Monte Carlo evaluation of calibration curves [77] and signed result
packages; the PDF reports and the SHA-256 digests provide integrity checks but not signatures. The Windows packages are
not yet code-signed. The project files contain the numerical data of the worked examples with a citation of their
source; the documents themselves are not distributed. The use of the program in laboratories has not yet been studied.

## 9. Conclusions

KAPSAM implements the GUM suite and the main sector guides in one engine and is verified on 286 projects from the
worked examples of 37 sources, with a documented tolerance for each of 1865 reference values, a test of the
tolerances under other seeds, and comparisons with an independent program, with spreadsheet formulas and with Monte
Carlo propagation. Recomputing the worked examples produced 100 findings; eleven change a final printed result, five of
them by more than a tenth of its expanded uncertainty, and a person who had not seen the code reproduced them. Most
findings are intermediate rounding or misprints that do not affect the measurement but make the examples unreliable as
references to their printed digits. Worked examples should therefore be published with unrounded, machine-readable
inputs and be recomputed by independent software before publication; the library of this work is a starting point.

## Appendix A. Equations not stated in the guidance

**A.1 Second-order terms.** With the gradient c, the Hessian H = (∂²f/∂xᵢ∂xⱼ) and the covariance matrix Σ of the
inputs at their estimates, the estimate of the 2026 amendment of the GUM [6] is y = f(x) + ½ tr(HΣ). For jointly normal
inputs the variance is u²(y) = cᵀΣc + ½ tr(HΣHΣ) + Σᵢⱼₖₗ cₗ f₍ᵢⱼₖ₎ Σₗᵢ Σⱼₖ, where f₍ᵢⱼₖ₎ are the third derivatives
(Mana and Pennecchi [41]); for independent inputs this is the expression of the note to GUM 5.1.2. The higher-order
part is given infinite degrees of freedom.

**A.2 Effective degrees of freedom with an ensemble.** The components uᵢ(y) = cᵢu(xᵢ) of inputs that are not in an
ensemble enter the Welch–Satterthwaite formula individually. The inputs of an ensemble E, estimated from the same data
with ν_E degrees of freedom, enter as one component u_E²(y) = Σ_{i,j∈E} cᵢcⱼu(xᵢ, xⱼ):
ν_eff = u⁴(y) / (Σ_{i∉E} uᵢ⁴(y)/νᵢ + u_E⁴(y)/ν_E), at most Σνᵢ with the ensemble counted once (Willink [50], the GUM
Tree Calculator [25]). GUM-5 eq. (2.3), used in Example 2, is ν_eff = u⁴(y) / Σᵢ [cᵢu(xᵢ) Σⱼ r(xᵢ, xⱼ)cⱼu(xⱼ)]²/νᵢ;
for one ensemble of two components with equal shares and r = 0.5 it gives 3.6ν where the ensemble has ν.

**A.3 The bootstrap of the NIST Consensus Builder.** For K results with weights wᵢ = 1/uᵢ² and Sₘ = Σwᵢᵐ, the moment
estimate of the between-laboratory variance is τ²_M = (Q − (K − 1))/(S₁ − S₂/S₁), which is negative when Q < K − 1;
the DerSimonian–Laird estimate is max(0, τ²_M). NICOB's source code draws Q from a gamma distribution whose mean and
variance are evaluated at the untruncated τ²_M, so that the mean equals the observed Q, with
Var Q = 2(K − 1) + 4(S₁ − S₂/S₁)τ² + 2(S₂ − 2S₃/S₁ + S₂²/S₁²)τ⁴; the linear term of Biggerstaff and Tweedie [60],
eq. (7), is 4(S₁ − 2S₂/S₁ + S₃/S₁²)τ². With τ̂ > 0 the two readings change u by 0.1 %; on a homogeneous set
(Q < K − 1) by 13 %. The program follows the code and keeps the published form as an option.

**A.4 Bayesian grids.** With known uᵢ, a flat prior on μ and a half-Cauchy prior on τ with scale s (the median of the
prior, set as in NICOB), μ is integrated analytically and the posterior of τ is evaluated on a grid uniform in the
prior probability, τ = s·tan(πθ/2), θ ∈ (0, 1), on which the half-Cauchy prior is constant. The grid covers all
τ ∈ [0, ∞); with a flat prior on μ the likelihood falls as τ^−(K−1), so the last cell carries a negligible mass for
K ≥ 2. Doubling the grid (10⁵ to 2 × 10⁵ points; for Laplace effects 300² to 600² points on (θ, μ)) changes means,
standard deviations and quantiles by less than 10⁻³ (Gaussian) and 10⁻² (Laplace) of the posterior standard deviation.
