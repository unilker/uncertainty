## Abstract

Laboratories accredited to ISO/IEC 17025 evaluate measurement uncertainty with spreadsheets or with programs that
cover one part of the GUM suite, and they learn and validate their methods on the worked examples of guidance
documents. We present KAPSAM, an open-source program with a Rust engine, a command-line tool and an application for
desktop and mobile platforms, that implements the GUM including its 2026 amendment, the Monte Carlo methods of JCGM 101 and
102, conformity assessment, top-down evaluation, proficiency-testing statistics, calibration with uncertainty in
both variables, interlaboratory precision, control charts, uncertainty from sampling, weighing instruments,
consensus values and results near a physical bound, with reports in English and Turkish. It is validated on 286
projects built from the worked examples of 38 documents: the test suite recomputes all 1865 reference values at
every change, an independent program, Suncal, reproduces the results of 171 of them, and spreadsheet formulas
recalculated in LibreOffice those of 189.
Recomputing the examples produced 100 findings, among them 13 confirmed inconsistencies that change a final printed
result. The choice of consensus procedure changes the degrees of equivalence of a key comparison as much as its
reference value. For laboratories, the program writes a software validation report for ISO/IEC 17025 and contains
a guide that explains every example step by step. We argue that worked examples should be published with
machine-readable inputs and checked by independent recomputation.

**Keywords:** measurement uncertainty; GUM; Monte Carlo method; software validation; worked examples; consensus
value; ISO/IEC 17025

## 1. Introduction

ISO/IEC 17025 requires testing and calibration laboratories to evaluate the uncertainty of their results (7.6) and
to validate the software they use for it (7.11.2) [1]. The evaluation itself rests on the *Guide to the expression of
uncertainty in measurement* (GUM) and its supplements: the law of propagation of uncertainty (LPU) [2], the Monte
Carlo method for one output (JCGM 101) [3] and for several outputs (JCGM 102) [4], the use of uncertainty in
conformity assessment (JCGM 106) [5], and, since 2026, an amendment of the GUM on nonlinear models [6] and a new
part with worked examples (GUM-5) [7]. Sector guides translate this framework: the Eurachem/CITAC guides for
analytical chemistry [8, 9], the Nordtest handbooks for environmental laboratories [10, 11], EA-4/02 [12], UKAS
M3003 [13] and the EURAMET calibration guides [14] for calibration, and ISO standards for proficiency testing [15],
precision experiments [16, 17] and gas analysis [18].

Each of these documents teaches by example. A laboratory that sets up a budget for a new method compares its
numbers with a worked example, and a laboratory that validates its software reruns worked examples whose results
are printed. Both uses assume that the printed results follow from the printed inputs. In our experience, and in
that of the developers of other programs (Section 6.4), they often do not exactly, and occasionally they do not at
all.

The software landscape reflects the structure of the guidance. The NIST Uncertainty Machine [19] evaluates
explicit models by the GUM and by Monte Carlo; Sandia's Suncal [20] adds symbolic LPU, curve fitting and decision
risk for calibration; the GUM Tree Calculator [21] and METAS UncLib [22] propagate "uncertain numbers" through
programs; the NIST Consensus Builder (NICOB) [23] combines interlaboratory results; the MUPR spreadsheet [24]
implements a top-down evaluation for chemistry. A survey of 35 programs by the European Metrology Network Mathmet
found that only 54 % give some evidence of validation and that support for JCGM 102 is the largest gap [25]. None
of these tools covers bottom-up and top-down evaluation, proficiency testing, conformity assessment and consensus
values together, and none publishes a validation that recomputes the worked examples of the guidance it
implements.

This paper makes four contributions, which also give its structure.

1. **The program, KAPSAM** (Sections 2 and 3): an open program that covers the GUM suite and the main sector guides, cites
   the clause behind every algorithm, and is built so that every result can be traced to its inputs, its method
   and the exact build of the software.
2. **Its validation** (Sections 4 and 5): a library of 286 self-checking projects from the worked examples of 38
   documents, with a documented tolerance for every one of 1865 reference values, an independent benchmark and a
   pre-registered classification of discrepancies.
3. **What the recomputation revealed** (Sections 6 and 7) about the examples themselves, and, for consensus values
   and results near a physical bound, how choices that the guidance leaves open change the reported result.
4. **Its use in laboratories** (Section 8): how the program serves the requirements of ISO/IEC 17025 on
   uncertainty, reporting and software validation, and how its guide makes the library a teaching resource.

## 2. Evaluation methods

### 2.1 Overview

A project contains the input data of one of eleven evaluation types (Table 1). Each type implements the cited
clauses of its guidance document; where a document leaves a choice open, the choice is an option of the project and
is named in the report.

**Table 1.** Evaluation types and the methods they implement (checked against the module documentation and
`docs/research/iso17025-mapping.md`).

| Project type | Methods | Main references |
|---|---|---|
| model | LPU of first and second order with correlations and ensembles; Welch–Satterthwaite; Kragten's spreadsheet; Monte Carlo (adaptive, shortest and symmetric intervals, half-width about the estimate); validation of the GUM result; bounded outputs | [2, 3, 6, 26] |
| multivariate | LPU and Monte Carlo for several outputs, explicit and implicit models, coverage regions | [4] |
| conformity | specific and global risks, guard bands (JCGM 106; test uncertainty ratio), lognormal processes, multicomponent items | [5, 27] |
| topdown | precision and recovery from validation and QC data (Nordtest, MUPR, ISO 21748) | [9, 10, 28] |
| proficiency | robust statistics, scores, homogeneity and stability (incl. a Bayesian between-unit model) | [15, 29] |
| calibration | generalised least squares with uncertainty in both variables | [18] |
| precision experiment | repeatability and reproducibility with outlier tests | [16, 17] |
| control chart | X- and R-charts, limits, rules, long-term review | [11] |
| sampling | duplicate method (classical ANOVA; range and log-transformed variants), fundamental sampling error; the robust ANOVA of the guide is not reproducible from its text (F63) | [30] |
| error function | weighing instruments, error approximation and uncertainty in use | [14] |
| consensus | DerSimonian–Laird with parametric bootstrap, Linear Pool, maximum likelihood, Bayesian random effects (Gaussian or Laplace); degrees of equivalence (MRA, leave-one-out, bilateral) | [23, 31, 32] |

### 2.2 Measurement models and the GUM

Models are written as expressions; the parser differentiates them symbolically, so that sensitivity coefficients
are exact and can be printed. The combined standard uncertainty includes correlations; the second-order term of
the estimate introduced by the 2026 amendment of the GUM [6] is always computed and added on request, and the
second-order variance terms for correlated normal inputs follow Mana and Pennecchi [26]. Inputs evaluated from the
same data can be declared as an *ensemble*, which enters the Welch–Satterthwaite formula as one component with its
common degrees of freedom (Willink [33]; the GUM Tree Calculator implements the same rule [21]). Expanded
uncertainties are rounded by the rule of EA-4/02 (ordinary rounding unless it lowers U by more than 5 %, then
upwards) [12], by the rule of the Eurachem/CITAC MUPR guide [9] or always upwards; the statement names the rule.
Calibration reports can apply the calibration and measurement capability as the smallest reportable uncertainty
(ILAC P14 [34]).

### 2.3 Monte Carlo method

The adaptive procedure of JCGM 101 7.9 stops on the stated numerical tolerance. Besides the probabilistically
symmetric and the shortest coverage interval, the program reports the half-width of the interval symmetric about
the estimate, which corresponds to the definition of the expanded uncertainty proposed by the JCGM working groups
(D61). The GUM result is validated against the Monte Carlo result (JCGM 101 clause 8); when inputs from repeated
observations are present, the report states that the two evaluations use different input variances for few
degrees of freedom (s/√n against √(ν/(ν − 2))·s/√n), so that a failed validation need not indicate an error (D85).

### 2.4 Consensus values and degrees of equivalence

The consensus module follows the NIST Consensus Builder [23, 32] and was checked against its R source code, which
is public. The DerSimonian–Laird estimate [37] is given with the Knapp–Hartung adjustment [38] and with the
parametric bootstrap of NICOB, which draws the between-laboratory variance from the gamma approximation of
Cochran's Q of Biggerstaff and Tweedie [39]. Reading the code showed two details that the manual does not state:
the gamma is set at the untruncated moment estimate of τ², which is negative when Q < n − 1, and its variance
differs in the linear term from the published formula; with τ̂ > 0 the difference is 0.1 % in u, with Q < n − 1 it
is 13 % (D82). The program follows the code and keeps the published form for reference.

The hierarchical Bayesian model with known u_j and a half-Cauchy prior on τ is evaluated without MCMC: μ is
integrated analytically and the posterior of τ is evaluated on a grid uniform in the prior probability,
τ = s·tan(πθ/2), on which the half-Cauchy prior is constant. For Laplace-distributed laboratory effects the normal–
Laplace convolution is written in closed form and the posterior is evaluated on a (θ, μ) grid. The results do not
depend on a seed; doubling the grid changes means, standard deviations and quantiles by less than 10⁻³ of the
posterior standard deviation (D83). Degrees of equivalence are available for the DerSimonian–Laird, Linear Pool
and Bayesian procedures, in the version of the CIPM MRA (D_j = x_j − μ̂) and in the leave-one-out version
(D*_j = x_j − μ̂₋ⱼ), unilateral and bilateral (D86, D88).

### 2.5 Results near a physical bound

A mass fraction cannot exceed 1 g/g. When y ± U crosses such a bound, the program truncates the interval at the
bound and states the result as y +U_U/−U_L, as the IUPAC technical report on purity assignment allows [40]; with the
Monte Carlo method it also reports the result restricted to the bound — the draws outside are discarded, which
corresponds to a rectangular prior on the measurand [41] — with its estimate, standard deviation and coverage
interval (D87).

## 3. Software design and implementation

### 3.1 Architecture

KAPSAM (Turkish for *coverage*, as in coverage factor and coverage interval) is organised around one engine, so
that every interface gives the same numbers (Figure 1). The engine
is a Rust library (`core`, about 13 000 lines) with a project layer (`project`, about 19 000 lines) that reads
project files, runs the evaluations, checks results against reference values and writes reports. A command-line
tool (`cli`) serves batch work and automation; a C interface (`ffi`) connects the engine to the application, which
is written in Flutter (about 9 000 lines of Dart) and runs on Windows, Linux, macOS, Android and iOS. Rust was
chosen for memory safety and for the speed that Monte Carlo evaluations with 10⁶ to 10⁷ trials need; Flutter
gives one user interface for all platforms. The program makes no network connections: all calculations run on
the user's computer.

<!-- fig1 -->

### 3.2 The project file

A project is a JSON file that holds everything needed to repeat an evaluation: bilingual metadata (title,
description, explanation, notes and sources with section and link), the input data of one evaluation type and,
optionally, expected values with tolerances and the reference they come from. The file of the first Eurachem
example, shortened:

```
{ "format": "uncertainty-calculations/project", "version": 1,
  "metadata": { "title": { "en": "Preparation of a cadmium calibration standard", "tr": "…" },
                "sources": [ { "document": "EURACHEM/CITAC Guide CG 4 (QUAM:2012.P1)",
                               "section": "Example A1, Tables A1.2 and A1.3" } ] },
  "model": { "output": { "name": "c_Cd", "unit": "mg/L", "expression": "1000 * m * P / V" },
             "inputs": [ { "name": "P", "distribution":
                             { "type": "rectangular", "value": 0.9999, "half_width": 0.0001 } },
                         { "name": "m", "unit": "mg", "distribution":
                             { "type": "normal", "mean": 100.28, "sd": 0.05 } }, … ] },
  "analysis": { "gum": { "coverage_factor": 2 }, "mcm": { "trials": 1000000, "seed": 1 } },
  "expected": { "gum": { "standard_uncertainty": { "value": 0.8633, "tolerance": 0.001 } } } }
```
The schema rejects unknown fields, so that a misspelt option is an error rather than silently ignored. Results are
never stored in the file; they are recomputed from the inputs. With its expected values, a project is its own test
case: `kapsam check` evaluates it and compares every result with its reference. This format is also our
proposal for publishing worked examples (Section 9).

### 3.3 Numerical implementation

The expression language provides the usual arithmetic, powers and elementary functions; derivatives of first and
second order are formed symbolically and simplified before evaluation. The random-number generator is the enhanced
Wichmann–Hill generator recommended by JCGM 101 [3, 35], checked against its primary source; normal deviates come
from the Box–Muller transform and gamma deviates from the method of Marsaglia and Tsang [36]. With a given seed a
Monte Carlo evaluation is exactly repeatable. Bayesian consensus values are evaluated on deterministic grids
(Section 2.4), so that they need no seed at all. All calculations are done in double precision; values are rounded
only in the reported statement.

### 3.4 Quality assurance of the software

- **Tests.** 218 automated tests of the engine include one that evaluates all 286 library projects and compares
  their 1865 results with the reference values; 28 tests of the application exercise its editors and results with
  the engine. They run at every change of the source code (continuous integration on GitHub Actions), together
  with a formatting check, a static analysis that treats every warning as an error, and the recalculation of the
  spreadsheet verification workbooks of the library in LibreOffice Calc (Section 4.4).
- **Packages.** A second workflow builds the packages for the five platforms from the same commit and runs the
  application tests against the engine on Windows and macOS before packaging. The Windows executable is built
  reproducibly, so that the same sources give a byte-identical file.
- **Identity of a result.** Every report states the program version, the git commit and date it was built from,
  the compiler, and the SHA-256 digest of the project as evaluated. A result can therefore be traced to its inputs
  and to a validated build (ISO/IEC 17025 7.5, 7.11).
- **Traceability of the development.** The repository contains a research log of every design decision (D1–D94)
  and finding (F1–F100), the generators that write each library project from the numerical data of its source, and
  the scripts of every independent recomputation (`tools/research/`). Licensed documents are used only for their
  numerical data and are not distributed.

### 3.5 User interface and reports

The application opens on cards for the eleven evaluation types and on the library, which can be searched and
filtered by level. A project has five tabs: an overview with the explanation of the project, an editor for the
model or the data, the settings (coverage factor or probability, Monte Carlo trials, rounding), the project file
itself, and the results (Figure 2). Every text of the interface and of the reports exists in English and Turkish,
and numbers follow the convention of the language (decimal point or comma). Reports are written as Markdown,
self-contained HTML with SVG charts, PDF (A4, fonts embedded) and JSON with all values in full precision.

## 4. Validation design

### 4.1 The library

The library holds 286 projects from 38 documents: the GUM and its supplements and GUM-5, the Eurachem/CITAC,
Nordtest, EA, UKAS and EURAMET guides, five ISO standards, NIST TN 1900 [42] and the Uncertainty Machine manual,
the EMPIR EMUE compendium [43], the IUPAC brief guide [44], the BIPM qNMR reference data reports [45], the
consensus papers of Koepke et al. [32] and Merkatas et al. [46], and the books and papers of Possolo and
co-authors [47, 48]. By field, 144 projects are in chemistry, 36 in electrical, 31 in mass, 24 general, 16 in
physical quantities, 12 in length, 10 in temperature, 7 in flow and 6 in pressure; by evaluation type, 134 are
measurement models (118 with a Monte Carlo evaluation) and 152 cover the other ten types (Table 2; the full list is
supplementary material S1).

<!-- table2 -->

### 4.2 Reference values and tolerances

A reference value is the value printed in the source, with a tolerance of half a unit in its last digit, wherever
the source reproduces it from its own inputs within its rounding (D15). Where the printed value is itself a Monte
Carlo or MCMC estimate, its sampling error is added to the tolerance. Where a source prints nothing, or prints a
value that does not follow from its inputs, the reference value is an independent recomputation (numpy/scipy
scripts in `tools/research/`) and the discrepancy is recorded as a finding; an engine's own output is never used
as its own reference. Monte Carlo results of the program are compared with a tolerance of about five times their
measured dispersion.

Of the 1865 reference values, 938 have a tolerance of half a unit in the last digit, 244 of up to one unit, 281
are Monte Carlo values and 402 have a wider tolerance whose reason is stated in the project (for example a value
derived from an input printed with two significant digits). Across all values, |Δ|/tolerance has a median of 0.23
and a 99th percentile of 0.98 (`numbers.md`).

### 4.3 Classification of discrepancies

Before the audit of the findings, the classes were fixed (D12, D73): (a) intermediate rounding that changes a
printed digit; (b) a misprint or transcription error without effect on the result; (c) an inconsistency; (n) no
discrepancy (a methodological note or a full reproduction). After an internal pre-review (Section 9) the class-c
findings were further divided by their effect (D84): c1, a final printed result — the estimate, its uncertainty or
the stated outcome — is not reproduced at its printed digits; c2, only an intermediate value is not; m, a
difference of method or interpretation rather than an inconsistency; e, an error already corrected by a published
erratum. A finding counts as confirmed when the recomputation from the document's own data leaves no open
question, or when it was reproduced independently; findings that await a check against another source are not
counted.

### 4.4 Independent checks

Every project that Suncal 1.7.1 can express was evaluated by both programs (D74, D93; supplementary material S4):
135 measurement models, 24 of them with correlated inputs or intermediate quantities; 12 models with several
outputs (JCGM 102); 6 calibrations with uncertainties in both variables, compared with Suncal's York fit for
straight lines and its orthogonal distance regression for the other functions; and 18 conformity assessments,
compared by the conformance probability of each result and the global consumer and producer risks. The other
project types (top-down evaluation, proficiency testing, precision experiments, control charts, sampling,
consensus values) have no counterpart in Suncal.

For measurement models, proficiency tests, precision experiments and top-down evaluations, KAPSAM also writes a
spreadsheet verification workbook (.xlsx; D94). It holds the data of the project and ordinary spreadsheet formulas:
the measurement model as a formula, evaluated at the estimates and at xᵢ ± 0.001 u(xᵢ), so that the sensitivity
coefficients are central differences in the spirit of Kragten's spreadsheet [8, Appendix E]; Algorithm A of ISO 13528 with one
column per iteration and the stopping rule of C.3.1 as a formula; the analysis of variance of ISO 5725-2; the
components of Nordtest TR 537 and their combination at each level. KAPSAM's own results appear only as fixed
numbers beside the spreadsheet's, with the difference and a tolerance. The arithmetic is thus done by the
spreadsheet program, and every formula can be inspected cell by cell; the formulas themselves, however, follow the
same reading of the standards as the program (Section 9). Every workbook of the library is recalculated in
LibreOffice Calc at every change of the source code (supplementary material S7).

## 5. Validation results

All 1865 reference values of the 286 projects are reproduced within their tolerances. The validation matrix
(supplementary material S3) records 228 worked examples or variants as reproduced, 25 of them with a finding.
Against Suncal (S4), the first-order standard uncertainties of the 135 measurement models agree to a relative
6.4 × 10⁻¹², and the estimates are identical except in the two projects that request the second-order estimate
term of the 2026 amendment (Δy = 0.5u and 0.1u by design; Suncal is first order). For the models with several
outputs the standard uncertainties agree to 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁴ and the correlation coefficients to 3.1 × 10⁻¹⁴. In the
79 Monte Carlo comparisons the standard deviations differ by at most 0.29 %; divided by the standard error of the
difference — which combines the sampling errors of both programs and a correction for the kurtosis of the output —
the largest |z| is 2.4, which for 79 independent comparisons has a probability of 0.75, so the dispersion is
consistent with sampling error alone. The calibration parameters agree to 2 × 10⁻⁴ of their standard
uncertainties; their standard uncertainties differ by up to 0.75 % when the fit is poor (largest weighted deviation
Γ above 1), because Suncal's covariance is the Gauss–Newton approximation while KAPSAM propagates the input
uncertainties through the minimum with the full Hessian (ISO 6143, Annex A); computed at KAPSAM's solution, the
Gauss–Newton values agree with Suncal's to 6 × 10⁻⁵. The 32 conformance probabilities and global risks agree to
1.4 × 10⁻¹⁰. The 189 verification workbooks of the library (135 measurement models, 39 top-down evaluations, 9
proficiency tests and 6 precision experiments), recalculated in LibreOffice Calc, reproduce all 4678 compared values
within their tolerances (S7); the largest difference is 0.44 of its tolerance, for the effective degrees of freedom
of M3003 Example K1, in which the central differences enter to the fourth power.

The consensus module reproduces the published DerSimonian–Laird, bootstrap, Linear Pool, maximum-likelihood and
hierarchical Bayesian values of Koepke et al. [32] for the Newtonian constant of gravitation and the half-lives
of ¹³⁷Cs and ⁹⁰Sr, of IUPAC 2024 [44] for nickel in bovine liver and the molar mass of iridium, of Possolo and Meija
[47] for the Hubble–Lemaître constant with Laplace effects (71.69 against 71.69 (km/s)/Mpc by Stan), and the

shows only in figures, an independent port of the NICOB R code to numpy serves as reference: the D values agree
exactly and the expanded uncertainties within 1.7 %, the Monte Carlo dispersion of 10⁵ draws.

## 6. What the recomputation revealed

### 6.1 Overview

Recomputing the examples produced 100 findings (F1–F100; all listed in supplementary material S2). By class, 27
are intermediate rounding, 25 misprints without effect, 23 inconsistencies, 13 no discrepancy, and 12 combine two
classes; 29 have a class-c component. Of these, 13 are confirmed inconsistencies that change a final printed
result (c1), 7 affect only intermediate values (c2, two of them partly corrected by errata), 5 are differences of
method or interpretation (m), 1 is a known erratum (e), and 5 are open and not counted (Table 4). The findings
concern most of the documents (Figure 3): the GUM-5 examples of 2026 carry 13, the Eurachem QUAM 11, UKAS M3003 and
the EMUE compendium 7 each (a finding with two classes counted in both).

### 6.2 Intermediate rounding is ubiquitous

Most documents round intermediate values — a sensitivity coefficient to three digits, a component to two — and
then combine the rounded numbers, so that the printed result differs from an exact recomputation in its last
digit. This is harmless for the result but makes the example unusable as a validation reference to the printed
digits, and it hides genuine slips among the rounding differences. Worked examples should state their inputs
unrounded and round only the reported result.

### 6.3 Spreadsheet budgets contradict themselves

The BIPM qNMR reference data reports print complete purity budgets from a common spreadsheet (F97). In 7 of 11
tables the printed uncertainty component of the integral ratio exceeds the product of the printed sensitivity
coefficient and standard uncertainty by 3–17 %, and the degrees of freedom of the ratio are fixed at 4 in five
tables. The certified values are not in question; the point is that a budget whose components cannot be recomputed
from its own columns cannot serve as a validation reference, and that recomputation from inputs finds this at once.

### 6.4 Monte Carlo and MCMC results are estimates

Printed Monte Carlo and MCMC values carry sampling error, and several discrepancies are of that size: a Bayesian
standard deviation of 0.19 a against the exact 0.185 a (F95), correlations from 5 × 10⁴ draws (F96), a bootstrap
standard uncertainty of 0.77 against 0.752 ng/g from 10⁶ replicates (F99). A deterministic evaluation, where one
exists, removes the ambiguity; where none exists, the number of draws should be printed with the result.

### 6.5 Method differences are not errors

Five class-c findings turned out to be differences of method or interpretation. In GUM-5 Example 2 the two areas
of analyte and internal standard come from the same three injections; their joint component of variance has two
degrees of freedom by Willink's ensemble rule [33], whereas the example applies a generalised Welch–Satterthwaite
formula and obtains ν_eff = 4.8 (F79). With ν fixed at those two values the GUM interval covers 89 % and 96 % in a
simulation of the measurement; with ν recomputed from each simulated data set, as a laboratory would, both readings
cover 93–94 %. The choice matters for the coverage factor of a single result (2.6 against 4.1) but neither reading
is an inconsistency. Two conventions for t-distributed inputs — the scaled and shifted t of JCGM 101 and the
rescaled t of TN 1900 and the NIST Uncertainty Machine — likewise give different Monte Carlo results for the same
data (F31, F35).

## 7. Choices that change the result

### 7.1 The consensus procedure is part of the model

For the 16 values of the Newtonian constant of gravitation analysed by Merkatas et al. [46], the program reproduces
DerSimonian–Laird with the Knapp–Hartung adjustment (6.673 99 × 10⁻¹¹ m³ kg⁻¹ s⁻², u = 0.000 25), maximum
likelihood (6.673 90, 0.000 25), the NICOB hierarchical Bayesian model (6.673 89, 0.000 27; exact grid instead of
MCMC) and the Linear Pool (6.673 77, 0.001 17); the fourteen procedures of the paper span 6.673 77–6.674 29 with
standard uncertainties from 0.000 11 to 0.001 17.

The degrees of equivalence depend on the procedure as much. For the 19 results of the ⁶⁰Co activity comparison in
the NICOB manual (participants coded), the number of pairs whose bilateral degree of equivalence differs
significantly from zero is 4 of 171 with DerSimonian–Laird in the MRA version, 9 in the leave-one-out version,
3 and 7 with the Bayesian model, and 22 with the Linear Pool in the MRA version, whose bilateral uncertainties
contain only the stated uncertainties of the two participants and not the dark uncertainty (Figure 4). The NICOB
code and manual differ in how the Linear Pool degrees of equivalence are computed (F100); the program implements
the manual. A participant's "significant" degree of equivalence is therefore a statement about the procedure as
well as about the result, and the procedure should be chosen and stated before the data are seen.

### 7.2 Results near a bound

For the purity of benzoic acid with an internal standard (BIPM ISRD-06, Table 6), y = 0.999 77 g/g and
U = 0.0015 g/g. Truncating y ± U at 1 g/g gives the printed 999.8 +0.2/−1.5 mg/g. The Monte Carlo result
restricted to w ≤ 1 g/g keeps 62 % of the draws and gives 0.999 32 g/g, u = 0.000 49 g/g and the shortest 95 %
interval [0.998 39, 1] g/g: a different estimate and a narrower interval. Both are defensible readings of the IUPAC
guidance [40]; the report should say which was used.

### 7.3 Guard bands from the test uncertainty ratio

In calibration, acceptance limits are often set from the test uncertainty ratio TUR = (T_U − T_L)/2/U₉₅. Taking
U₉₅ = 2u, as Suncal does, makes TUR about 2 % lower than U₉₅ = k₉₅u for large degrees of freedom and higher for
small ones; Dobbert's managed guard band gives acceptance limits outside the tolerance above TUR = 4.59 unless it
is limited (D80). The program uses k₉₅u by default, keeps 2u as an option and limits the factor to 1 unless an
agreed decision rule allows more.

### 8.2 Validation of the software and technical records

ISO/IEC 17025 7.11.2 requires a laboratory to validate the software it uses before use and after changes. The
command `kapsam check library --lab my-projects --report validation.pdf` writes a software validation report
that lists the program version, the commit, the compiler and the platform, the SHA-256 digest of every project file
and of the set, and every comparison with its reference value and tolerance; the laboratory's own projects appear
in a section of their own, because reproducing published examples verifies the implementation but not the
laboratory's procedure (supplementary material S5 is such a report). A laboratory keeps its typical projects with
their expected values and reruns them after every update of the program. For technical records (7.5) and data
integrity (7.11.3), the project file, the report and the digest printed in the report together document an
evaluation: anyone can later check that a filed report belongs to a given project file. With `--workbooks folder`
the same command writes the spreadsheet verification workbook of every project that has one, and the report lists
them with their digests and an empty column for the person who opens them in Excel to record the outcome; in the
application the workbook of the open project is one menu entry away (*Verify in Excel*). A laboratory thus checks
its own projects with its own spreadsheet program, which many assessors regard as the familiar form of software
validation.

### 8.3 Statements in reports and certificates

The reported statement gives the value, U, k and the coverage probability, names the rounding rule and, for
calibration, applies the CMC of the laboratory as the smallest reportable uncertainty (ILAC P14 [34]). A
conformity assessment states the decision rule, the guard band and the specific risk of each result, as 7.8.6
requires; the guard bands derived from the test uncertainty ratio are limited as described in Section 7.3. Results
near a physical bound are stated with an asymmetric interval (Section 2.5).

### 8.4 Learning with the program

Worked examples are also how uncertainty is taught. The Mathmet overview of training courses, software and
classroom examples [25] shows how closely the two are linked, and many laboratories train new staff with the
examples of the Eurachem and EA guides. The application therefore contains a guide, *Uncertainty from scratch*, in
English and Turkish: twelve chapters from the idea of uncertainty to reporting, including a first calculation step
by step, a table that turns certificates, tolerances, resolutions and repeated readings into standard
uncertainties, the reading of every part of the results, the choice of the project type and the most common
mistakes. Links in the guide open the corresponding library examples.

Every project, including the user's own, has an explanation in plain words that the program writes from the project
data: what is measured, what each input means and how its standard uncertainty follows from the given numbers
(for example U/k from a certificate or a/√3 from a tolerance), what the program calculates and which results the
source expects. The 286 library examples are grouped into 123 lessons that say what each group teaches, at three
levels (48 examples for beginners, 159 intermediate, 79 advanced), and the library can be filtered by level. The
library thus becomes a graded set of exercises with known answers.

## 9. Discussion

**Worked examples as validation data.** The examples of guidance documents are de facto the reference data of
uncertainty software, but they are published as typeset text. Converting them to machine-readable form and
recomputing them exposed the problems of Section 6, which careful reading alone had not caught. We propose that
issuing bodies publish the inputs of their examples in a machine-readable form — the project format of Section 3.2
is one candidate — and recompute them before publication. The library of this work is offered for that purpose.

**What this validation does and does not show.** Reproducing published values verifies the implementation of
documented methods; it does not validate a laboratory's own measurement procedure, which is why the validation
report keeps the laboratory's projects in a section of their own (Section 8.2). Agreement with the published values
can also hide a common-mode error when the program and the independent scripts read a standard the same way; the
Suncal benchmark, the NICOB source code and the NIST Uncertainty Machine are the truly independent checks. The
spreadsheet workbooks make the arithmetic independent of the program and every formula visible, but their formulas
were written from the same reading of the standards; a laboratory's own validated spreadsheet applied to the same
data would be a stronger check. An
internal pre-review was carried out with an AI assistant (Section 10); an independent review by metrologists
outside the project, including the recomputation of at least ten of the c1 findings by a person who has not seen
the code, is planned before submission.

**Reporting the findings.** The findings were not communicated to the issuing bodies before submission. The c1
findings of Table 4 will be sent to them as technical notes after acceptance, so that the notes can refer to a
peer-reviewed record, and their replies will be added to the public research log of the program.

**Limitations.** Not implemented are the NICOB priors on σ_j for results with finite degrees of freedom, the
degrees of equivalence with Laplace effects, time series (ARMA) and counting statistics, model averaging, and
signed result packages; the PDF reports and the SHA-256 digests provide integrity checks but not signatures. The
Windows packages are not yet code-signed, so endpoint protection may quarantine them as unknown files. The
interface, the reports and the guide exist in English and Turkish; further languages are possible but need review
by metrologists who are native speakers. The use of the program in laboratories has not yet been studied
systematically; a pilot with laboratories of the institute is planned. Licensed standards are used only for their
numerical data.
